Position

Masking is a skill and a cost. Who decides is the difference.

Masking is two things with one name.

One is a cost the room imposes. The other is a skill a person chooses. TƎT takes a position on both: the cost belongs to the room, the skill belongs to the person.

Below: what the research says, where TƎT is careful, three questions that tell the two apart, and what a team can do.

The cost is real

Autistic adults describe masking, or camouflaging, as hiding natural behaviour and performing someone else’s: copying speech and body language, rehearsing scripts, forcing eye contact. In one study of 92 adults, the most common consequence was exhaustion. People also described anxiety, constant self-monitoring, and a sense of not being their true self (Hull et al., 2017).

It can also hide people from support. Masking is linked to late or missed diagnosis, because the people around you do not see the difficulty (Hull et al., 2017; Pearson & Rose, 2021).

The same mechanism shows up well outside autism. A working paper reported by Fortune surveyed 300 college-educated Black employees about code-switching: adjusting how you speak, look and act to make others comfortable. They did it most when they wanted promotion, wanted to show they fit, or needed protection from discrimination, and the researchers describe a heavy psychological cost (McCluney et al., via Fortune, 2019).

In each of these, the pressure comes from the environment.

The skill is real too

TƎT’s Adaptive Masking is a narrower thing: the conscious adjustment of communication style, visible behaviour and self-presentation to the norms of a context, without losing your own substantive position. The Chameleon fable is about the moment that skill meets its limit: being asked to become several things at once.

Even the researchers most sceptical of masking draw this line. Pearson and Rose note that watching how you come across is a normal part of social life, and that ordinary shifts between contexts differ from masking because they do not mean hiding a true self. They also say that for autistic people the line can blur (Pearson & Rose, 2021).

The Antwerp Maritime Academy case shows the useful version: a concern that has to be translated across rank, language or register before anyone will hear it. Doing that well is a skill.

Where TƎT is careful

Two findings stop us from saying more than we can.

The choice is often not free

Pearson and Rose call the idea that masking is a free choice an illusion, and read it as self-protection against stigma, often with unconscious parts (Pearson & Rose, 2021). We take that seriously. TƎT calls adaptive masking a talent only where the choice is real. Where it is not, it is a load, and we call it that.

Choosing when to mask is not shown to be free

In a study of 262 autistic adults, people who masked heavily in every setting reported more stress than people who masked little. People who switched, masking in one kind of setting and not another, did not escape: the authors conclude that switching was about as stressful as masking all the time (Cage & Troxell-Whitman, 2019).

So we do not claim a chosen shift is free. We claim it is a different kind of thing from a demanded one, and that how much of it a person is asked to do matters.

Three questions

To tell the cost from the skill, we ask three things. They are TƎT’s working questions, not a validated measure, and nobody gets a score.

Ask these of a person or a team

Who decided?

Did the person choose this shift, or did the room demand it? A shift you chose and can drop is a skill. A shift you cannot drop is a load.

How often?

Fine once in a meeting. The Chameleon’s friction is the day a room asks for it twice in one day.

What does recovery cost?

Many people in the Hull study needed time alone afterwards. If a shift needs that, count it as a cost, however smooth it looked from outside.

What a team can do

Not “who is masking?” but “what is this room asking people to become?”

  • Count the shifts. How many registers, personas or translations does a normal week ask for, and from whom?
  • Make translation somebody’s job. In the maritime case a concern had to be translated to be heard. When translating is shared work, it stops falling on whoever happens to be best at it.

Limits

  • Hull et al. used a self-selected sample, mostly women. Pearson and Rose is a conceptual analysis, not a measurement. Cage and Troxell-Whitman report differences between groups, which cannot by themselves show what causes what.
  • The code-switching evidence is a working paper as reported in the press. We have read the report, not the paper itself.
  • TƎT applies the idea of masking beyond autism. The studies above do not test that.
  • The three questions are ours. They are prompts for a conversation, not tests.
