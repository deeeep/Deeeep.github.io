Position

Translating yourself for a room: a skill when chosen, a cost when demanded.

Every organisation runs on translation. The question is who pays for it.

TƎT proposes that when the room demands it, the organisation loses attention, voices and capability it never sees. Below: what we know, what we only propose, and where we stop.

The skill

TƎT synthesis, with an illustration

Contextual translation means fitting how you speak, what you show and how you frame a point to a room, while keeping your own position. A junior officer's safety concern has to be put in words a senior officer will act on. The Antwerp Maritime Academy case is built around that moment.

Done by choice, this is one of the eight talents TƎT names, Adaptive Masking. The fable that tells it is The Chameleon.

The cost

Emerging evidence

Researchers have studied what it costs when people hide their natural behaviour and perform someone else's. In a study of 92 adults (the group is named in the source title below), exhaustion was the most common consequence, and people described constant self-monitoring (Hull et al., 2017).

A conceptual analysis argues the choice is often less free than it looks, because the shift protects against stigma (Pearson & Rose, 2021). In a study of 262 adults, people who adjusted only in some settings reported about as much stress as people who adjusted everywhere (Cage & Troxell-Whitman, 2019).

Where this stops: all three studies looked at one group, and the first drew on a self-selected sample. TƎT applies the idea to any workplace. The studies do not test that.

What an organisation loses

Hypothesis, not measured

TƎT proposes three losses.

  • Attention. Effort spent on translating is not spent on the task.
  • Concentration. Whoever translates best becomes the permanent translator, and carries the load for everyone.
  • Silence. What cannot be translated is not heard. In the maritime case a concern failed until someone put it in the room's own words.

What cuts against this: in some roles translation is the job and costs little. We searched for studies that measure these three losses at organisation level and found none we have read. Treat them as questions to test, not findings.

Three questions

TƎT synthesis: prompts, not a measure

To tell the cost from the skill, ask these of a task or a team. Nobody gets a score.

Ask of a room

Who decided?

Did the person choose this shift, or did the room demand it? A shift you chose and can drop is a skill. One you cannot drop is a load.

How often?

Fine once in a meeting. The strain starts when a room asks for it again and again in one day.

What does recovery cost?

If a shift leaves someone needing time alone afterwards, count that as a cost, however smooth it looked from outside.

What a team can do

TƎT synthesis

Not “who translates well?” but “what is this room asking people to become?”

  • Count the shifts. How many registers or translations does a normal week ask for, and from whom?
  • Make translation shared work. When it is part of someone's role, it stops falling on whoever is best at it.
  • Change the room first. Ask what the room could do before asking what a person should do.

Boundaries

  • Skilled translation is never evidence that no adjustments are needed.
  • TƎT does not ask for translation, score it or reward it, in any activity.
  • TƎT does not replace any adjustment a person is entitled to.
  • TƎT is not used to select, rate, rank or assess individuals.
